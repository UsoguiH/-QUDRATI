# T3: the audience (Saudi students, 16–18, preparing for Qudrat quant)

*2026-10-28. Track 3 of the law3 research. It covers who the rule flow is for: how 16–18 year olds respond to reward, status and peers, what reads as childish to them, how time pressure interacts with maths anxiety, the Saudi context, and which social mechanics work without a server. It builds on `research/rule-study.md` and `research/rule-study-2.md`. Those two rounds used simulated AI personas, so their quotes are hypotheses to test, not evidence. Strength tags: **[strong]** = replicated or meta-analytic, **[moderate]** = one good study or consistent smaller ones, **[weak]** = correlational, company-reported or press, **[opinion]** = argument or design judgement.*

---

## A. TL;DR

1. At 16–18, reward and social-evaluation systems are at their most sensitive. Sensation seeking peaks around 19 in an 11-country sample, while self-regulation matures until the mid-20s. The "teen brain is broken" reading of this is over-sold. The useful reading is that **status, respect and peers move this age more than they move children or adults**.
2. The best-evidenced lever for this age is **respect**. Messages that treat teens as competent, autonomous people with high standards work, and adult-style instruction backfires (Yeager, Dahl & Dweck 2018; Bryan et al. 2016; "wise feedback", Yeager et al. 2014).
3. "Childish" means being talked down to, which includes over-praise, baby steps the student did not need, cartoon cheering and gendered pet names. "Cool" means competence, speed and insider status: «هذي اللي تطيّح الناس».
4. **Never put a visible countdown on first exposure to a rule.** Anxiety eats working memory (Ashcraft & Kirk 2001), and pressure hurts the strongest students most (Beilock & Carr 2005). Bring in exam pace later, as an opt-in round where a miss costs nothing.
5. Low-stakes retrieval *reduces* reported test anxiety: 72% of 1,408 students said it made them less nervous (Agarwal et al. 2014, self-report). Frequent, safe review is both the memory tool and the anxiety tool.
6. Growth-mindset slogans are hype-prone. The best national trial raised lower achievers' GPA by about 0.10 points, and only where peer norms supported it. **Show, don't preach:** give a precise diagnosis plus a new attempt, never a "believe in yourself" banner.
7. Strong and weak students need opposite things (the expertise reversal effect). Strong students need a skip-by-proof; weak ones need the worked steps. One flow should branch on a first answer, not on a self-label.
8. Saudi teens live on their phones. Mobile is 98–99% of internet access, Snapchat and TikTok reach about 70–80% of people, and peak use is 9–11 pm. Qudrat prep culture rewards «اختصارات/حيل», speed and score. The weighted-percentage stakes are real: ETEC's own table puts 81+ in the top 5%.
9. Without a server, use a **relative, ghost league** (only nearby ranks, never the bottom on display), plus **cooperative and share mechanics**: a spoiler-free result card to Snap or WhatsApp, and a "same 5 questions" challenge link. Public failure is costly at this age (Somerville et al. 2013), so keep misses private.
10. Copy should sound like a sharp older student. Use Saudi dialect, short lines, no exclamation storms and no emoji. Keep it gender-neutral by using first-person plural, noun phrases and the possessive «ـك», and by avoiding gendered imperatives and verbs ending in «ـي».

## B. Findings

| # | Finding | Evidence + strength | What it means for a rule screen or review card |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sensation seeking peaks in late adolescence, around 19, while self-regulation keeps improving to a plateau at 23–26. The pattern is similar across 11 countries. | Steinberg et al. 2018, *Dev Sci*, more than 5,000 people aged 10–30 **[strong]**. Saudi Arabia was not in the sample. | Novelty, a little risk and visible stakes motivate this group. Give a "bet" or a challenge round that is optional and cannot hurt progress. |
| 2 | The dual-systems / imbalance models (Steinberg; Casey) are contested. Their behavioural predictions are weakly supported, and reward-system activity is sometimes *adaptive*. | Pfeifer & Allen 2012; the "Mind the gap" review (PMC6969358); Crone & Dahl 2012 recast adolescence as **social-affective engagement + goal flexibility** **[moderate]** | Do not design around "teens are impulsive". Design around "teens re-prioritise goals fast when the goal feels like *theirs* and socially meaningful", which here means the score, the college and respect. |
| 3 | Peers watching raises adolescents' risk-taking and reward-region activity, but not adults'. | Chein et al. 2011, *Dev Sci*, fMRI, simulated driving **[moderate]**, a small lab study that is widely cited | Social presence amplifies whatever the screen rewards. If peers "see" anything, it should be effort and wins, never misses. |
| 4 | Peer observation can also help learning. 18–22 year olds watched by peers explored more, learned faster from positive *and* negative feedback, and performed better. | Silva, Shulman, Chein & Steinberg 2016, *J Res Adolesc*, n = 101 males **[moderate]** | A "friends are doing the same 5 questions" frame or a ghost rival may sharpen learning from feedback. It is worth an A/B test, not an assumption. |
| 5 | Being watched produces self-conscious emotion, which rises from childhood into adolescence, along with heightened arousal. | Somerville et al. 2013, *Psych Sci* **[moderate]** | Misses must feel private. No public "wrong" states. Share cards show bests only. A league never puts the student at the bottom of a visible list. |
| 6 | Adolescents are more sensitive than children to status and respect. Interventions fail when they feel like adult control, and succeed when they confer status or autonomy. | Yeager, Dahl & Dweck 2018, *Perspect Psychol Sci* (review) **[moderate]** | Frame rules as **insider knowledge** («اللي يعرفها يسبق»), not homework. Never "you must memorise". |
| 7 | Healthy eating reframed as rebellion against manipulative marketers (status, autonomy, justice) led 8th graders to choose fewer sugary snacks the next day than standard health lessons did. | Bryan, Yeager et al. 2016, *PNAS* (RCT); follow-up Bryan, Yeager & Hinojosa 2019, *Nat Hum Behav* **[moderate]** | Values framing transfers to study: «الاختبار مصمّم يطيّحك بالوقت — القاعدة هذي تقلب اللعبة». Use it sparingly or it turns cringe. |
| 8 | "Wise feedback" doubled essay revision among 7th graders who distrusted teachers (64% vs 27%). The feedback combined criticism with a statement of *high standards plus belief* that the student can meet them. | Yeager, Purdie-Vaughns et al. 2014, *JEP: General* **[moderate]**; the effect was smaller in the group that already trusted teachers | A miss card should say why it is strict and that the student can do it: «نشدّ عليك لأن هدفك عالي». Then show the exact gap. |
| 9 | Inflated praise ("incredibly good") *reduced* challenge-seeking in low-self-esteem children and increased it in high-self-esteem children. | Brummelman et al. 2014, *Psych Sci* **[moderate]**; the sample was children, not 16–18 | Use plain, specific praise («صح — ٤ ثوانٍ»). Superlatives hurt exactly the anxious students you most need to keep. |
| 10 | Expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (d about −0.28 to −0.40). Informational positive feedback does not. | Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, meta-analysis of 128 studies **[strong]** | Gems and XP should *inform* ("you're faster than last week"), not *pay* for each rule. Do not make rules feel like chores done for coins. |
| 11 | Teacher autonomy support predicts students' need satisfaction and autonomous motivation. | Bureau et al. 2022, meta-analysis of 144 studies, about 79,000 students **[strong]**, correlational | Give real choices: skip-by-proof, "show me why", "one more round". Use explanations, not commands. |
| 12 | Growth-mindset intervention: a short online module raised lower achievers' GPA by about 0.10 points in a US national sample of 12,000 9th graders, and only where peer norms supported it. Meta-analyses find tiny to null average effects once study quality is accounted for. | Yeager et al. 2019, *Nature* **[strong for "small and conditional"]**; Sisk et al. 2018 (mean d about 0.08); Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023, *Psych Bull* **[moderate]** (disputed by other meta-analysts) | No mindset slogans on rule screens. The "growth" message is carried by mechanics: new numbers after a miss, a visible personal-best time, missed rules coming back and being beaten. |
| 13 | Expertise reversal: guidance that helps novices *hurts* more knowledgeable learners, because it adds redundant load. | Kalyuga et al. 2003 and later work **[strong]** | Open each rule with one exam-style item. A right answer earns skipping the walkthrough («معروفة عندك؟ نتخطى»). A wrong answer, or «ما أعرف», gets the steps. This also answers «أنا ثالث ثانوي مو ابتدائي». |
| 14 | Teens (13–17) are impatient, read less, and dislike condescending or childish design. They like self-expression and sharing. | NN/g "Teenagers on the Web", about 100 participants **[moderate]**, usability research | Keep lines to one breath. No paragraphs. Make the rule the hero and the mascot the margin note. |
| 15 | Maths anxiety consumes working memory. High-anxiety students fail as memory load rises. | Ashcraft & Kirk 2001 **[strong]**; Barroso et al. 2021 meta-analysis, r = −.28 between maths anxiety and achievement **[strong]** | First exposure must be low-load: one idea, one picture, no clock, no hearts. |
| 16 | Pressure makes high-working-memory students "choke" to average on hard problems. | Beilock & Carr 2005 **[moderate]** | Pressure is not only a weak-student problem. Even the 85+ crowd learns better without a clock during learning. |
| 17 | Evidence that *time pressure* specifically causes maths anxiety is sparse and mixed. | Caviola et al. 2017, review of 19 studies **[moderate on "unknown"]**; claims that "timed tests cause anxiety" are mostly **[opinion]** | Pace can be trained. Bring it in late, opt-in, with the clock as a speed readout («٤٢ ثانية — أسرع من الاختبار») rather than a draining bar. |
| 18 | Frequent low-stakes retrieval: 92% said it helped them learn and 72% said it made them less nervous for exams. | Agarwal et al. 2014, n = 1,408 middle and high school students **[moderate]**, self-report | Review cards are the anxiety cure. Say so once: «كل مراجعة تخفّف توتر يوم الاختبار». |
| 19 | Reappraisal: being told that arousal *helps* performance raised GRE-maths practice scores and real GRE-maths scores 1–3 months later. Ten minutes of expressive writing before an exam lifted anxious students' grades. | Jamieson et al. 2010 **[moderate]**; Ramirez & Beilock 2011, *Science* **[moderate]**, with some replication debate | Before the first timed round, add one line: «التوتر يعني جسمك جاهز». | 20 | Gamification works on average: g about 0.5 for cognitive outcomes. Effects depend on design, and competition combined with collaboration does better than competition alone. | Sailer & Homner 2020, *Educ Psychol Rev* **[moderate]**; Roseth, Johnson & Johnson 2008, 148 studies on 12–15 year olds: cooperation beats competition on achievement **[strong]** | Mix in cooperation (shared goals, "help a friend's streak") with the league. Do not rely on the league alone. |
| 21 | Badges and leaderboards can *lower* intrinsic motivation and exam scores in a semester course. A leaderboard acts like an implicit "difficult goal". | Hanus & Fox 2015 **[moderate]**; Landers et al. 2015 **[moderate]** | Keep the league light and relative (nearby ranks only). Do not hang rule mastery on rank. |
| 22 | People ranked low on a leaderboard tend to disengage, while high performers are energised. | Several studies; the summary found is secondary **[weak]** | Ghost leagues should place the student where a climb is likely. Never show a long list below the student with the student last. Qudrati's climb already shows only a window. |
| 23 | Duolingo (company data): leaderboards raised lessons started and completed; a second streak freeze raised daily actives by 0.38%; learners with a friend streak were 22% more likely to finish the daily lesson; learners with friends were 5.6× likelier to finish a course. | Duolingo blog posts **[weak]**: A/B tests without detail, and the friend figures are correlational with obvious self-selection | A streak "shield" for one bad day is supported. ## C. Voice and tone guide for rule flows

**Who is speaking.** An older student who scored high last year. They are quick, dry and on the student's side, and they talk *with* the student, never down. فهيم can carry the voice, but he is a coach in the margin, not a cheerleader at the centre.

**Do**
- Talk about **the exam and the clock**, the shared enemy: «هذي اللي تطيّح الناس», «توفّر لك دقيقة».
- Give **specific, factual praise**: time, streak and what was mastered. One short sentence, one exclamation mark at most per screen.
- Make **diagnosis the kindness**: name the exact missing step («نسيت ÷ ٢»), then offer a new attempt with new numbers.
- Offer **choices** (autonomy): «معروفة عندك؟ نتخطى الشرح» / «ليش تشتغل؟» / «جولة ثانية؟».
- Say **why it is strict** (wise feedback): high standard + «تقدر عليها».
- Treat **coming back** as a fresh start, not guilt: lead with what is waiting, and keep it short.
- Keep it **gender-neutral**. Use «نـ» first-person plural («نكمل», «نشوف»), noun phrases («الجواب صح»), the written possessive «ـك» (it reads the same for both genders), and passive or impersonal forms. Avoid imperatives and second-person verbs that mark gender («خذ/خذي», «حليت/حليتي»).
- Use Eastern Arabic digits and real numbers (seconds, days to the exam), never vague hype.

**Don't**
- Use baby talk or pet names: «يا بطل», «يا شاطر», «يا صغيري», «برافو عليك يا حلو».
- Inflate praise: «عبقري!!!», «واو أنت أذكى واحد!», «مذهل مذهل!». The anxious students are the ones it pushes away (finding 9).
- Celebrate a non-answer or a timeout («تخمين حلو» after nothing registered), or say «خلص الوقت» during learning.
- Use mindset slogans: «آمن بنفسك», «لا تستسلم», «الفشل طريق النجاح».
- Use guilt or fear on return: «فهيم زعلان منك», «بتخسر كل شي».
- Over-explain what the student already did right, or re-teach ٩ × ٥ right after showing it.
- Hint at leaked or real exam questions («من التجميعات», «جات في الاختبار»). It breaks the "original questions" rule and ETEC's rules.
- Use emoji, Latin digits, or the «·» separator.

**Ten example lines** (Saudi dialect, gender-neutral, no emoji)

| Moment | Line | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Right answer, fast | «صح — ٤ ثوانٍ. الاختبار يعطيك دقيقة.» | Specific, ties speed to the real exam, no superlative |
| Right on a trap item | «هذي اللي تطيّح ناس كثير — وعدّت.» | Insider status; the question is the subject, so no gender |
| Miss, diagnostic | «قريب. الناقص خطوة وحدة: ÷ ٢ — كل زوج انحسب مرتين.» | Names the gap and keeps it private and calm |
| Miss, wise feedback | «نشدّ عليك هنا لأن هدفك عالي — وهذي قاعدة تنمسك. نجرّبها بأرقام ثانية.» | High standard + belief + immediate retry |
| Skip offer for strong students | «لو القاعدة معروفة عندك: سؤال واحد صح ونتخطى الشرح.» | Autonomy, expertise reversal, no gendered imperative |
| Rule reveal | «القاعدة اللي توفّر دقيقة في أسئلة المجاميع:» | Value first, in exam terms |

## D. Three personas (grounded in the findings above; names are illustrative)

**1. ريم — the ambitious one (scientific track, target 85+ for medicine).** Top of her class and on TikTok daily for «حيل قدرات» clips. She already knows half the rules and is offended by baby steps («أنا ثالث ثانوي مو ابتدائي»). She is motivated by status: beating the clock, ranking in the top three, knowing the trap before others do (findings 1, 6, 28). Hidden risk: she can choke under pressure on hard items (16), and inflated praise makes her coast. *Serve her:* an exam-style opener that lets her skip by proof (13); trap discrimination («أيّ قاعدة؟»); personal-best times; the climb; a share card worth posting. *Avoid:* forced walkthroughs, and «يا بطلة».

**2. فيصل — the average, anxious one (scientific track, aiming for about 75).** He uses Snapchat in the evenings and tried a paid course but zoned out. He knows he is "not a maths person" and freezes when a bar drains: one 6-second timer, and he quits (15, 31). He will do a lot if it feels safe and short. *Serve him:* no clock or hearts on first exposure; a diagnostic miss followed by a retry with new numbers (8); frequent low-stakes review, framed as the thing that lowers exam nerves (18); an opt-in pace round later with a reappraisal line (19); a streak shield (23). *Avoid:* superlatives (9), public misses (5), «لا تستسلم».

**3. سارة — the weak, disengaged one (theoretical track, quant is 25% of her score, low maths base).** She has had a weak maths base since middle school (29). She is on TikTok and games for hours and opens the app because a friend sent a link. She will leave in the first minute if it feels like school, or if it makes her feel stupid. *Serve her:* the picture-first "why" (the staircase that becomes a rectangle); two easy wins in the first three minutes; a dignified step-back; cooperative hooks such as "same 5 questions as your friend" and a shared goal (20); evening nudges (26). *Avoid:* the league as her first contact (22), long rule text (14), and anything that reads as remedial.
